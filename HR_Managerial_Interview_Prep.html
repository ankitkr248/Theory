            I'm a Senior Software Engineer with 4+ years of experience building scalable backend systems in Java and Spring Boot, currently at Jubilant FoodWorks. I own end-to-end delivery of core logistics and platform systems — most recently the Delivery Management System, which handles rider allocation, order clubbing, and payouts for 400K+ daily orders across 25K+ riders.

            02What are your current responsibilities?▸
            • Owning end-to-end design and delivery of backend microservices in Java/Spring Boot, from architecture through production rollout
            • Making system design decisions for new features — data modeling, failure handling, scaling strategy
            • Building and maintaining APIs/services handling high-volume operational data (GPS, orders, payouts)

            • Handling production incidents and post-incident follow-up

            03What project are you most proud of?▸

            The Delivery Management System (DMS) rider allocation and payout engine. The challenge was building allocation logic that stayed correct and fast at scale — ~58 GPS updates/sec flowing through Cassandra, Kafka, and MQTT — while supporting anti-spoof geofencing and dual routing providers with automatic failover.

            I designed allocation as a chain of 9 rule classes and built the payout engine around 18+ incentive types composed with 20+ eligibility handlers across 4 Kafka consumers. What I'm proudest of isn't just the accuracy improvement (35%) — it's that we replaced opaque payout rejections with rider-facing non-eligibility remarks, which directly reduced support escalations and improved trust in the platform.

            04Tell me about a challenging problem you solved▸

            On the SSO/IAM platform, the challenge was migrating 120+ services from duplicated per-service auth logic to a centralized system without downtime or breaking live traffic. A hard cutover was too risky given the blast radius.

            I designed a three-mode rollout filter — off, shadow, and enforce — that let new auth logic validate silently against live production traffic before it started gating real requests. I paired this with an S3-backed offline decision cache so IAM outages wouldn't take down dependent services. This let us de-risk the rollout incrementally and catch discrepancies before they became incidents, and the resulting S2S auth library cut integration effort for other backend teams by 80%.

            05How do you handle production issues?▸
            • Assess impact first — which services/users are affected, and how severely
            • Root-cause using logs, dashboards, and traces — I don't guess; I look at what actually broke
            • Mitigate or roll back immediately if there's a safe fast path to restore service
            • Fix the root cause properly and deploy after testing — the rollback buys time, it isn't the fix
            • Post-incident review — what alerting/logging gap let this reach production, and what would catch it earlier next time

            On the DMS routing layer, this pattern is literally built into the system — a circuit breaker automatically fails over from OSRM to Google Routes, which cut downtime by 40% without needing a human in the loop for the common case.

            06How do you handle disagreements in a team?▸

            I focus on the technical trade-off, not the person. I listen to the reasoning first, then evaluate options against concrete criteria — latency, maintainability, blast radius, how reversible the decision is. I bring data or a prototype where possible rather than arguing from opinion. If we're stuck, I default to the tech lead's call rather than let it become a stalemate — speed of decision matters more than winning the argument.

            14Describe a situation where a production issue occurred and how you handled it▸
            SITUATION

            The routing layer for rider allocation depended on a single external routing provider; any provider degradation risked stalling allocation for thousands of riders.

            TASK

            Make the system resilient to routing-provider failures without manual intervention.

            ACTION

            I put OSRM as the primary provider and Google Routes as a fallback, wrapped both behind a circuit breaker so failures triggered automatic failover rather than cascading into allocation delays.

            RESULT

            This cut downtime by 40% and removed a single point of failure from a system processing hundreds of thousands of orders daily.

            15Tell me about a complex system you designed or improved▸
            SITUATION

            Delivery ETAs relied on a single end-to-end estimate, which didn't reflect where time was actually being lost (prep vs. transit vs. last-mile).

            TASK

            Build a more accurate, explainable prediction system feeding the customer-facing delivery promise.

            ACTION

            I built an ONNX Runtime inference service around a multi-output regression model that split the prediction into prep, transit, and last-mile segments, and mirrored the offline Python training pipeline feature-for-feature (haversine distance, cyclical time encoding, per-store encoding, a festival calendar) so training and serving stayed consistent.

            RESULT

            85–95% per-segment accuracy at 500 orders per batch call, now feeding SLA reporting — and the approach won our company-wide hackathon in 2026.

            16How do you ensure reliability in backend services?▸
            • Fault tolerance by design — timeouts, retries, circuit breakers on every external dependency (this is literally how the DMS routing failover works)
            • Concurrency safety — optimistic-lock retries and atomic conditional updates so two writers can never both "win" (used in both the Loyalty ledger and the Job Scheduler)
            • Observability — structured logging, metrics, and alerts so issues surface before customers notice
            • Testing discipline — unit/integration tests, with an 80% coverage gate enforced on the DMS
            • Safe rollout — shadow-mode validation against live traffic before a change is allowed to affect real requests (used in the SSO rollout)

            System Design & Technical Depth

            5
            25How do you handle high-traffic backend systems?▸
            • Stateless services behind load balancers for horizontal scaling
            • Data-layer optimization — indexing, query tuning, Redis caching to keep the database off the hot path
            • Async processing for anything non-critical-path, via Kafka/queues
            • Strong observability so traffic spikes are visible before they become incidents

            On the DMS, this combination is what lets us sustain ~58 GPS updates/sec across 25K+ riders without allocation lag.

            26How do you design scalable microservices?▸

            Clear domain boundaries (payments, rider management, IAM each own their data), stateless services for horizontal scaling, event-driven communication to reduce coupling, and per-service database ownership. I add circuit breakers and retries at every service boundary so failure in one service degrades gracefully instead of cascading — this is the same principle behind the DMS's dual-routing-provider failover.

            27How do you ensure data consistency in distributed systems?▸

            It depends on the operation's criticality. For payments, I lean on strong consistency and transactional guarantees. For most workflows, I use eventual consistency via event-driven updates, backed by idempotent APIs (so retries can't double-process) and reliable message delivery through queues. The Loyalty ledger is a good example — transfers redeem the source card and mint a new one under optimistic-lock retry, so concurrent wallet writes can't corrupt state even without a full distributed transaction.

            28How do you handle real-time updates like rider status or notifications?▸

            Backend services publish state-change events (location, delivery status, assignment) into a stream, which decouples producers from consumers and lets the system scale independently on each side. For direct real-time channels, I've built this at the transport layer too — the Data Call service uses a WebRTC signalling layer over Netty-SocketIO for offer/answer/ICE relay, with an Opus-only, video-disabled profile to keep it viable on constrained mobile data, which cut telecom costs by 60% (~INR 1.3 crore/year) for 50K+ users.

            29Tell me about a system you built that impacted business metrics▸

            The distance-based payout and incentive system. Before it, payout calculations were opaque and needed manual reconciliation by operations. I built configurable slab-based payout logic with auditable aggregation and dashboard APIs, which improved rider-earnings transparency, cut manual reconciliation effort, and let ops teams adjust payout rules without a code deploy — directly improving both operational efficiency and payment accuracy (35%).

            Commonly asked in senior-level HR/managerial rounds. Prepare short outlines rather than memorized scripts for these.

            35Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information▸

            Frame this around a real ambiguity — e.g., defining payout edge cases for multi-order deliveries before all operational rules were finalized. Emphasize: you made a reasonable default assumption, built in configurability so it could change without a redeploy, and validated with stakeholders early rather than waiting for perfect clarity.

            36How do you prioritize when you have multiple competing deadlines?▸

            Talk about assessing business impact and blast radius first (a production-facing payout bug vs. a nice-to-have dashboard feature), communicating trade-offs to stakeholders early rather than silently slipping a deadline, and negotiating scope before negotiating timeline.

            37Tell me about a time you had to give difficult feedback to a peer or teammate▸

            Keep this behavioral and STAR-based — focus on being specific and behavior-based rather than personal, timing the feedback close to the event, and following up to confirm it landed constructively.

            38How do you approach learning a new technology or unfamiliar part of the codebase quickly?▸

            You could reference the ML delivery-prediction project — going from an unfamiliar ML-serving stack (ONNX Runtime, XGBoost) to a production inference service by first understanding the offline training pipeline in detail before writing serving code, to avoid train/serve skew.

            39Describe a time you had to push back on a deadline or scope▸

            Use a real or illustrative example: propose a phased rollout (like the shadow-mode SSO rollout) instead of an all-or-nothing deadline, so you protect reliability without simply saying "no."

            40How do you handle ambiguity in requirements from product/business teams?▸

            Emphasize collaborative clarification: ask targeted questions to convert ambiguity into concrete edge cases (e.g., defining payout rules for multi-order deliveries with product/ops), propose a reasonable default, and build in configurability so business logic can evolve without engineering being the bottleneck.

            41What's a technical decision you'd make differently if you could redo it?▸

            Prepare a genuine, specific example — reviewers respond well to real self-awareness here. A good structure: what you chose, why it seemed right then, what you learned, and what you'd do differently (or what safeguard you now build in by default because of it).

            42How do you stay current with new technologies and engineering practices?▸

            You can honestly mention your use of AI-assisted development tools (Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot) as part of your daily workflow — for code generation, review, and test scaffolding — as an example of adopting new tooling deliberately rather than resisting it, plus reading engineering blogs / following system design discussions relevant to your domain.

            43Tell me about a time you had to say no to a stakeholder request▸

            Good opportunities: pushing back on frontend-only business logic (see Q18) or negotiating phased rollout instead of a risky big-bang deploy. Frame it as protecting a shared long-term interest (maintainability, reliability), not just personal preference.
